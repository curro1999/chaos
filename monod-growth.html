The Monod equation

Around 1940, in wartime Paris, Jacques Monod was growing bacteria in flasks and timing how fast they multiplied. He found that the growth rate depends on how much food is left, and that the dependence follows a simple curve: fast while the nutrient is plentiful, slowing down only when it is nearly gone. He summed it up in one equation:

μ = μmax · S / (Ks + S)μ is the growth rate, S is the concentration of the limiting nutrient

The same experiments led him to diauxie, the two-step growth of bacteria fed two sugars, and from there to the discovery of how genes are switched on and off, for which he shared the 1965 Nobel Prize. The equation is still the starting point for modelling a fermentation.

What the values mean

X · biomass
The amount of cells in the culture, in grams of dry weight per litre.
S · nutrient
The concentration of the one nutrient that limits growth, usually the sugar, in grams per litre.
μ · growth rate
How fast the biomass is growing at this moment, per hour. A value of 0.5 means the biomass is growing at 50% per hour at that instant.
μmax · maximum growth rate
The growth rate when the nutrient is unlimited. It belongs to the organism and the conditions: temperature, pH, medium.
Ks · half-saturation
The nutrient concentration at which growth runs at half of μmax. A small Ks means the cells keep growing at full speed until the nutrient is almost gone. Monod measured values of a few milligrams per litre, far smaller than this slider allows, which is why real cultures stop so abruptly.
Y · yield
Grams of biomass made per gram of nutrient consumed.
S₀ and X₀
The nutrient and the biomass at time zero, when the flask is inoculated.
Final biomass
What is left when the nutrient is gone: X₀ + Y · S₀.
Fastest doubling time
The time the culture needs to double while growing at μmax: 0.693 divided by μmax.
Nutrient runs out after
The time at which less than 1% of the starting nutrient remains.
Top chart
The two equations of a batch culture solved together: dX/dt = μ X and dS/dt = −μ X / Y. The biomass draws the familiar growth curve and the nutrient mirrors it.
Bottom chart
The equation itself. It has the same shape as the Michaelis–Menten curve for enzymes, though Monod arrived at it by fitting his growth measurements.

Lower Ks to 0.01The growth curve stops with a sharp corner. The cells do not notice the shortage until the very end.
Raise Ks to 5Growth slows down long before the nutrient is gone and the curve bends gently.
Double S₀The final biomass doubles, but the culture needs only about one more doubling time to get there.
Raise X₀The whole curve shifts to the left. A bigger inoculum saves time and changes little else.
