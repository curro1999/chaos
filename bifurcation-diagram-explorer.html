Period doubling and chaos

xnext = r · x · (1 − x)

Robert May's 1976 paper in Nature, “Simple mathematical models with very complicated dynamics”, followed this one population rule as a single number, r, is turned up. The population first settles, then alternates between two values, then four, then eight, and finally never repeats at all.

The diagram below is that whole story in one picture. Every horizontal position is a value of r. The dots above it are the values the population keeps visiting once it has settled down.

What the values mean

r · horizontal axis
The growth parameter: how many times the population would multiply each generation if nothing held it back.
x · vertical axis
The population as a fraction of the maximum the environment can hold, from 0 (extinct) to 1 (full).
Blue dots
For each r the rule is run for 500 generations, which are thrown away, and the next 260 values are plotted. One dot above an r means a steady population. Two dots mean it alternates. A vertical smear means chaos.
Orange line and dots
The r you have chosen and the values the population visits there. Use the slider or drag across the diagram.
Bottom chart
The population over 80 generations at the chosen r. The heights it ends up at are the orange dots in the diagram, seen as a sequence in time.
Behaviour
Whether the population settles, repeats in a cycle, or never repeats, checked by running the rule for several thousand generations.
Values visited
How many different values the population cycles through in the long run.

What to try

  1. Drag from 2.5 to 3. There is one line, so the population settles on one value. The bottom curve flattens.
  2. Cross 3. The line forks in two and the bottom chart becomes a zigzag: a high generation, a low one. This is the first period doubling.
  3. Continue to about 3.45, then 3.55. Each branch forks again, giving 4 values and then 8. Notice that each fork arrives sooner than the last.
  4. Press Period-doubling cascade. The forks crowd together like a countdown. Each stretch is about 4.669 times shorter than the one before. That ratio is the Feigenbaum constant, and it turns up in many unrelated systems. Infinitely many doublings fit before r ≈ 3.570.
  5. Press Full view and look to the right of 3.57. The cloud is chaos: the population never repeats. It is not uniform, though. There are pale vertical gaps.
  6. Press Period-3 window. Near 3.83 the chaos suddenly gives way to a clean cycle of 3 values. Each of the three branches then forks into 6, 12 and so on, replaying the whole diagram in miniature.
